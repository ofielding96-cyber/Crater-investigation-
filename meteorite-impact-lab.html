Welcome to the virtual laboratory. You will investigate how the height a meteorite is dropped from affects the size of the crater it makes. You will plan a fair test, collect results, plot a graph and write a scientific conclusion, all on your own.

How do craters form on the Moon?

Space is full of rocks called meteoroids. When one hits the Moon at tens of thousands of kilometres per hour, its movement energy is transferred to the surface in a fraction of a second. Rock is pushed out and thrown away, leaving a bowl-shaped crater with a raised rim.

1. Approach
A meteoroid speeds towards the surface. The Moon has no air to slow it down.
2. Impact
Its kinetic energy is transferred to the ground in an instant.
3. Excavation
Material is thrown outwards, forming a bowl and a raised rim.
4. Settling
Dust and rocks settle. The crater stays for a very long time.

Why can we still see craters that are millions or billions of years old? The Moon has almost no atmosphere, no wind or rain, no flowing water and no moving tectonic plates, so there is nothing to wear craters away. On Earth, weather, oceans, plants and moving plates hide them.

Important: real meteorites hit at 11 to 72 km/s. In this lab you drop a model meteorite from a few tens of centimetres into flour, at speeds of only a few metres per second. This is an analogy to help us understand the idea. The numbers come from a computer model; they are not measured from real impacts.

AResearch question

How does the height from which a meteorite is dropped affect the diameter of the crater it produces?

Variable definitions
Independent variable: the one thing you change. Dependent variable: the thing you measure. Controlled variables: things you keep the same so the test is fair.

CMethod

  1. Choose Drop height as the variable you are investigating in the lab below. The meteorite size, flour surface, flour depth (5 cm) and 90° vertical drop are locked.
  2. Set the drop height to 20 cm and press Drop Meteorite.
  3. Read the crater diameter from the ruler (or let the lab record it).
  4. Repeat for 40, 60, 80 and 100 cm. Use all five heights.
  5. Check that every result is in your table, then plot your graph.
  6. Optional: switch on Repeat Trials to drop each height three times, so you can calculate a mean.
